Australia's Shoebox Goldmine

How sports trading cards became a global market, and where Australia sits in it.

How to read the search charts: Google Trends doesn't count searches. It scores each place or week from 0 to 100, where 100 is the highest share of all Google searches that were about "sports cards". A small state can score higher than a big one.

Where in the world?

Americans search for sports cards more than almost anyone. Australia scores 20 out of 100, behind Canada, the UK and New Zealand.

Interest stayed flat from 2021 to 2024, then took off. The yearly average jumped from about 25 in 2024 to 76 in 2026, and searches peaked in June 2026.

Zoom into Australia

Australia followed the global boom almost week for week. Interest stayed flat for four years, then jumped in late 2025 and peaked in mid-2026.

Splitting each year into its own panel shows the same story. 2021 to 2024 look almost identical, 2025 climbs at the end, and 2026 sits far higher than every year before it.

Australians search most in winter (June to August) and again in December, when cards become Christmas presents.

South Australia leads the country, and smaller states like Tasmania and the NT search more than NSW and Victoria.

To be worth serious money, a card has to be "graded". An expert checks its condition and seals it in a plastic case with a score out of 10. All 9 Australian grading dealers we found send cards to companies in the United States, and every one of them uses PSA in California.

Grade decides the price

At the top of the market, prices are huge. The 14 biggest publicly reported sales all topped US$2 million, and three passed US$10 million.

Age isn't everything. A Honus Wagner card printed over 110 years ago sold for US$7.3 million, but so did modern cards only a few years old, like Shohei Ohtani's 2026 card, which sold for US$11 million in the same year it was printed.

Most record cards were graded by the same three US companies Australian dealers use: PSA, BGS and SGC.

The takeaway

Sports cards went from a hobby to a global market in five years, and Australians joined in at the same time as everyone else. But every card worth real money still has to make a trip to America first. That old shoebox in the cupboard might be worth checking.
